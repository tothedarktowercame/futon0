# Batch C — Environment / sandbox

Encoding date: 2026-08-20. Each `knowable-at-T` snapshot is restricted to the
organisation's founding plus approximately one year. Later population and
outcome evidence is kept outside that snapshot.

## Mojang (Minecraft)

**case-id**  
`mojang-minecraft`

**org**  
Mojang Specifications, founded by Markus Persson in 2009 around development of
Minecraft. [M1]

**period**  
2009 -> Microsoft acquisition, 2014. [M2]

**node-types**  
Primary: **Environment** — Minecraft is an open-world sandbox without one
official end; players bring construction, exploration, survival, social, and
server goals into it. [M3]  
Secondary: none. Multiplayer servers and modifications can become dependencies
in other creations `[recollection - unverified]`, but that is not Mojang's
primary payer relation.

**chain**  
Minecraft sandbox executable/world rules -> Mojang sponsorship -> players buy
copies and later platform versions/content `[recollection - unverified]` ->
`terminal{interest}` in play, creation, exploration, and shared activity; no
external obligation requires a player to buy Minecraft. [M3] Microsoft's later
acquisition payment is a separate shareholder exit chain based on its interest
in the franchise and community; Microsoft described more than 100m PC downloads
and billions of Xbox hours when announcing the deal. [M2]

**oracle**  
The non-manufacturable check was whether people entered the world, invented
their own projects, shared creations, played together, and returned. Minecraft's
lack of an official end makes internally generated goals observable rather than
an inference from a fixed campaign. [M3] Purchase counts and active play are
mechanical proxies, while the variety/value of player goals is interpretive.
[inference from M2][M3]

**oracle-hardness**  
**Mechanical** for purchases, downloads, and activity; **interpretive** for
whether the environment supports intrinsically valuable user-created goals.
[inference from M2][M3]

**distance**  
**Direct / 1 hop**: access to the sandbox -> paid game copy. Minecraft's public
alpha reportedly generated enough sales for Persson to leave his job and fund
Mojang. [M1]

**fixture-access**  
**y**, once the public alpha was available: a newcomer could buy/download and
attempt self-directed play without Mojang selecting them. [M1] This contradicts
the schema's claim that every Environment test requires permission. It requires
a population, but a public self-serve population can arrive without prior
permission. [inference from M1]

**knowable-at-T**  
T = approximately May 2010, one year after the first public alpha. A blind scan
could see a small, repeatedly updated block-building/survival sandbox that had
become popular on forums, with paid-alpha revenue beginning to exceed its
creator's employment income. [M1] It could see that users, rather than a authored
campaign, supplied much of the activity. [inference from M3] The visible test
was continued purchasing, creating, sharing, and returning; the scan could not
presume a full release, console ports, mass adoption, or acquisition.

**outcome**  
**scaled** — before its 2011 full release, Minecraft beta reportedly passed
16m registrations and 4m purchases; Microsoft acquired Mojang/Minecraft in
2014 and described more than 100m PC downloads. [M2][M4] The schema's single
outcome cannot also encode the acquisition; capture was scaled before the
`acquired-sideways`-like exit, and the acquisition was of the core environment
rather than a sideways artifact. [inference from M2][M4]

**evidence-refs**

- [M1 — Mojang/Minecraft founding, early forum popularity, and paid-alpha revenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_of_Minecraft%3A_Java_Edition)
- [M2 — Microsoft's 2014 acquisition announcement and usage figures](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2014/09/15/games-minecraft-to-join-microsoft/)
- [M3 — official Minecraft description as open-world sandbox without an official end](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/about-minecraft)
- [M4 — Minecraft beta registrations and purchases before full release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minecraft)

## Roblox Corporation

**case-id**  
`roblox-corporation`

**org**  
Roblox Corporation, founded in 2004 by David Baszucki and Erik Cassel. [R1]

**period**  
2004 -> scaled public company, 2021-26. [R2][R3]

**node-types**  
Primary: **Environment** — users play, communicate, and create millions of 3D
worlds and virtual items inside the platform. [R2]  
Secondary: **Dependency** — creators depend on Roblox Studio/runtime,
distribution, identity, and economy for their experiences. [inference from R2]

**chain**  
Roblox creation/play platform -> Roblox Corporation sponsorship -> players buy
virtual currency/access and brands or advertisers buy platform exposure
`[recollection - unverified]`; creators receive a share of platform economics
`[recollection - unverified]` -> `terminal{interest}` in play, social connection,
creation, audience, or promotion. No external obligation to use Roblox was
found. The platform's user-generated worlds and items establish that the
artifact's value depends on participant goals rather than a fixed Roblox-authored
task set. [R2]

**oracle**  
The non-manufacturable check is whether creators build experiences and players
choose them, socialize, spend, and return. Roblox reports daily-active-user and
developer/community measures in securities filings; the company defines DAU
counting rules, making activity measurable though not fully outside its control.
[R2][R3]

**oracle-hardness**  
**Mechanical** for logged activity, transactions, and creator output;
**interpretive** for whether those activities constitute durable community and
creative value. [inference from R2][R3]

**distance**  
**Direct / 1 hop** for player spending on the environment
`[recollection - unverified]`; **2 hops** for creator-made experience -> player
engagement/spending -> Roblox platform share `[recollection - unverified]`.
The second route shows why an Environment can capture value from transactions
over user-generated goals without owning each goal.

**fixture-access**  
At founding/T, **n**: the first couple hundred builders were developers,
investors, testers, and founders' friends rather than an open population. [R1]
After public launch, **y**: Roblox became free to play. [R1] Population was
required, but permission was a bootstrapping choice rather than a permanent
property of the node.

**knowable-at-T**  
T = approximately 2005. A blind scan could see an in-development 3D
construction/physics environment seeded with a few hundred developers,
investors, testers, and friends. [R1] It could observe whether this invited
population built things and returned, but there was not yet a large public
population on which to test the broader platform hypothesis. [inference from R1]
The scan could not assume the 2006 public release, a child audience, virtual
economy scale, mobile distribution, or public listing.

**outcome**  
**scaled** — Roblox reported an average 49.5m daily users in its 2021 annual
filing, participating in millions of community-built worlds, and remains a
public reporting company. [R2][R3] Its latest filing also states a history of
net losses, so scaled adoption/capture does not imply sustained profitability.
[R3]

**evidence-refs**

- [R1 — official company information: 2004 founding and first few hundred builders](https://en.help.roblox.com/hc/en-us/articles/203313370-Roblox-Company-Information)
- [R2 — Roblox 2021 annual filing: founding vision, user-generated worlds, and DAU scale](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000131509822000058/rblx-20211231.htm)
- [R3 — Roblox 2025 annual filing: corporate status, DAU definition, and net-loss warning](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000131509826000024/rblx-20251231.htm)

## Linden Lab (Second Life)

**case-id**  
`linden-lab-second-life`

**org**  
Linden Lab, founded by Philip Rosedale in 1999; Second Life launched in 2003.
[L1]

**period**  
1999 -> open, 2026. Second Life reported roughly 750,000 monthly active users
at its 20th anniversary in 2023. [L2]

**node-types**  
Primary: **Environment** — residents build spaces, goods, identities,
communities, performances, and commercial activities in an open-ended virtual
world. [L1][L2]  
Secondary: none. The internal marketplace resembles a market, but **Market** is
not among schema v1's node types; Environment already captures the primary
user-goal direction.

**chain**  
Second Life world/creation tools/economy -> Linden Lab sponsorship -> residents
pay memberships, land charges, and transaction/economy-related charges
`[recollection - unverified]` while other residents buy user-created goods and
services -> `terminal{interest}` in identity, sociality, creation, events, and
virtual commerce. [L2][L3] No external obligation to inhabit Second Life was
found. Early business shifted from paid memberships toward land sales. [L3]

**oracle**  
The non-manufacturable check is whether residents create places and activities
that attract other residents, transact, and persist without Linden Lab specifying
their goals. [L2] Registration, concurrency, active use, creations, and
transactions are mechanical proxies; meaningful community and creator welfare
remain interpretive. [L2][L4]

**oracle-hardness**  
**Mechanical** for activity/economy counts; **interpretive** for whether the
world's resident-generated social and creative value is healthy. [L2]

**distance**  
**Direct / 1 hop** from environment access/land to Linden Lab charge
`[recollection - unverified]`; **2 hops** from resident creation -> another
resident's transaction -> Linden Lab's economy/land capture
`[recollection - unverified]`. The large resident economy is not equivalent to
Linden Lab revenue, a distinction the schema does not directly encode. [L2]

**fixture-access**  
At founding/T, **n** because no public Second Life environment existed. Closed
beta began in 2002; public beta followed in 2003. [L3] At launch, enrollment
became public/self-serve `[recollection - unverified]`. Again, population and
permission are separate variables.

**knowable-at-T**  
T = approximately 2000. A blind scan could see a company founded to develop a
new form of shared 3D entertainment/experience. [L1][L5] It could inspect a
technical vision or prototype `[recollection - unverified]`, but could not run
the decisive Environment test because there was no public resident population.
The first closed beta arrived in 2002, and Second Life launched in late 2003
with about 500 residents and a one-square-kilometre world. [L3][L4] Those later
facts are cited here only to bound how long the population test took, not to
backfill T with its outcome.

**outcome**  
**open** — Second Life survived for at least twenty years and reported roughly
750,000 monthly active users, 1.6m daily transactions, and a $650m resident
economy in 2023. [L2] This is enormous sustained environment value but does not
establish comparable capture by Linden Lab; private-company revenue and
profitability are `[recollection - unverified]`. Therefore `scaled` would
silently conflate resident economic volume with sponsor capture.

**evidence-refs**

- [L1 — Linden Lab official history: 1999 founding and 2003 Second Life launch](https://lindenlab.com/about)
- [L2 — official 20th-anniversary population, creation, and economy figures](https://lindenlab.com/press-release/original-metaverse-second-life-celebrates-20th-birthday)
- [L3 — Second Life history: beta dates and early membership/land business model](https://wiki.secondlife.com/wiki/History)
- [L4 — Linden Lab testimony: approximately 500 launch residents and later population](https://www.congress.gov/event/110th-congress/house-event/LC9120/text)
- [L5 — 2002 beta announcement describing the shared-3D-environment thesis](https://wiki.secondlife.com/wiki/Second_Life_in_2002/PR)

## PlanetMath

**case-id**  
`planetmath`

**org**  
PlanetMath, a free collaborative online mathematics encyclopedia started in
2001. [P1]

**period**  
2001 -> inactive by approximately 2018/currently. [P2][P3]

**node-types**  
Primary: **Environment** — contributors brought their own mathematical concepts,
expositions, corrections, and discussions into an interlinked encyclopedia;
the project supplied authorship, ownership, review, and linking machinery. [P1]
Secondary: **Call** — grants and sponsored development were possible funding
routes, but no specific founding-year call was established. [P4]

**chain**  
PlanetMath encyclopedia/Noosphere collaboration environment -> PlanetMath.org
nonprofit and volunteer community sponsorship -> donors, sponsors, or grant
funders were possible payers -> `terminal{interest}` in free mathematical
knowledge, pedagogy, peer review, and open resources. [P1][P4] No regulation,
accreditation, contract, or named recurring grant obligation requiring PlanetMath
was found. At the time of its administration document, it reported no permanent
paid staff and listed donations, sponsorship, grants, and limited sales as
possible revenue streams. [P4]

**oracle**  
The non-manufacturable check was whether mathematicians contributed rigorous
entries, linked and corrected one another's work, and continued maintaining the
collection. [P1] Article counts, edits, and active contributors would be
mechanical, but reliable historical population series could not be established
from the sources reviewed `[recollection - unverified]`.

**oracle-hardness**  
**Interpretive** — mathematical correctness, pedagogy, and community maintenance
require judgment even where contribution/activity counts are mechanical. [P1]

**distance**  
**Far / at least 2 hops**: contributor-created encyclopedia value -> public
educational use/community legitimacy -> voluntary donor, sponsor, or grant
budget. [inference from P4] There was no direct user-payment line and no
permanent paid staff in the cited administration account. [P4]

**fixture-access**  
**y** — the environment was a free collaborative encyclopedia, so a newcomer
could read and, subject to account/authorship rules, contribute without sponsor
selection. [P1] The first population reportedly included members of the
Undernet `#math` channel, but that origin is `[recollection - unverified]` here
because this research did not locate a strong English primary source for it.

**knowable-at-T**  
T = approximately 2002. A blind scan could see a free, community-driven
mathematics encyclopedia created after MathWorld went offline during litigation,
with interlinked LaTeX mathematical entries and an ownership model in which an
article author acted as gatekeeper while others proposed corrections. [P1] It
could observe whether the initial community contributed and reviewed entries,
but reliable T-year user/article counts and funding records were not established
`[recollection - unverified]`. It could not assume institutional hosting,
software migrations, a 2,300-page snapshot, or eventual inactivity.

**outcome**  
**zombie** — the public record describes PlanetMath as inactive, while its
freely licensed content remains available and a large encyclopedia snapshot was
preserved. [P2] A secondary-language history reports portal operations ending
in 2018 and source content moving to GitHub, but that date was not independently
confirmed from a primary announcement. [P3] `failed` would erase the surviving
knowledge artifact; `sustained-noncommercially` would overstate present community
activity.

**evidence-refs**

- [P1 — PlanetMath FAQ: 2001 origin, collaboration goals, and authorship model](https://planetmath.org/theplanetmathfaq1)
- [P2 — PlanetMath public summary: nonprofit ownership, free content, preserved book, and inactive status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlanetMath)
- [P3 — German PlanetMath history reporting 2018 portal shutdown/content preservation](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlanetMath)
- [P4 — PlanetMath administration document: nonprofit status, no permanent staff, and possible revenue streams](https://planetmath.org/planetmathadministrationmaindocument)

## GNU Emacs / Free Software Foundation

**case-id**  
`gnu-emacs-fsf`

**org**  
GNU Emacs, begun by Richard Stallman in 1984 and widely distributed in 1985;
the Free Software Foundation was founded in October 1985 to support GNU
development. [E1][E2]

**period**  
1984/85 -> sustained noncommercially, 2026. GNU and FSF continue to publish and
support Emacs. [E3][E4]

**node-types**  
Primary: **Environment** — users bring editing, programming, writing, mail,
planning, debugging, and other goals into an extensible Lisp environment and
can reshape it with their own extensions. [E5][E6]  
Secondary: **Dependency** — personal and organisational workflows/configurations
depend on Emacs and its extension interfaces `[recollection - unverified]`, but
there is no open-core capture path.

**chain**  
GNU Emacs executable/source/extension environment -> GNU Project and FSF
sponsorship plus volunteer maintainers -> historically purchasers of tapes and
manuals, and donors/supporters of FSF -> `terminal{interest}` in software freedom,
cooperative development, documentation, and a user-controlled computing
environment. [E2][E3] The terminal is explicitly noncommercial: the FSF exists
to advance user freedoms, and GNU software may be redistributed whether or not
a price was paid. [E1][E4]

**oracle**  
The non-manufacturable check is whether users adopt Emacs for their own work,
write and share extensions, and keep maintaining the environment across changing
systems. Emacs's architecture was explicitly designed so unrelated user
extensions could compose, and GNU's history says growing user interest helped
prompt formation of the FSF. [E2][E6]

**oracle-hardness**  
**Interpretive** — downloads, contributions, packages, and continued releases
are mechanical proxies, but there is no single score for the variety or value
of user-owned workflows. [inference from E5][E6]

**distance**  
For environment use, **none to sponsor revenue**: anyone may copy, modify, and
redistribute GNU software. [E1] Historical capture was **2 hops**: Emacs use and
community interest -> demand for convenient distribution/manuals or commitment
to software freedom -> FSF tape/manual sale or donation. [E2] The artifact's
value and sponsor funding are intentionally decoupled.

**fixture-access**  
**y** — source and redistribution freedom let a newcomer attempt Emacs without
FSF permission. [E1] As with Minecraft, the Environment test needs users but
does not intrinsically require permission; free redistribution deliberately
removes that gate. [inference from E1]

**knowable-at-T**  
T = approximately 1985-86. A blind scan could see GNU Emacs becoming usable and
widely distributed, users asking to obtain it, and an extensible editor whose
commands and user additions were written through the same Lisp machinery.
[E2][E6][E7] Growing demand supported creation of the FSF and a tape-distribution
and manual-sales operation. [E1][E2] The scan could test user adoption and
extension sharing, but could not assume decades of maintenance, GNU/Linux
distribution, modern package ecosystems, or breadth beyond editing.

**outcome**  
**sustained-noncommercially** — GNU Emacs remains maintained and distributed as
free software, while FSF's terminal is its nonprofit mission and supporter
base rather than licence rent from Emacs users. [E3][E4][E5] This is not “no
capture”: donations, historical media/manual sales, volunteer work, and
institutional stewardship sustain it, but the captured resource is not
proportional payment for environment value. [E2][E4]

- [E3 — FSF history and continuing organisational home for GNU](https://www.fsf.org/history)
